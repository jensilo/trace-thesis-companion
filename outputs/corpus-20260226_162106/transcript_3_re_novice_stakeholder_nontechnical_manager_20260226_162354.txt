Stakeholder: We want to reduce manual work so staff can focus on higher-value things, cut down the frequency of student-facing scheduling errors so trust goes up, and make reporting straightforward so leadership can see progress. The usual failures are double-booked students, last-minute changes that don’t propagate, and people at sites not getting the updated lists. We get a fair number of student complaints each term about conflicting assignments or missing clinical placements. It’s time-consuming to reconcile and it embarrasses us with students.

Stakeholder: We can’t do multi-day onboarding for everyone. It needs to be short — like, people should be comfortable with core tasks after a single day of training.

Stakeholder: Things like enrolling or moving a student into a cohort, assigning a student to a clinical site, generating a roster, and sending notifications. Not every edge case — just the day-to-day stuff.

Interviewer: And how will you know someone is “able to use” the system after that training? Any measures?

Stakeholder: Hmm, I think we’d do a checklist or a quick observation. If they can complete a handful of common tasks without help that’s good enough. We don’t need a formal exam, just a practical thumbs-up.

Interviewer: Understood. On to scheduling rules — what kinds of conflicts or constraints do you need the system to catch or prevent?

Stakeholder: It should stop the obvious stuff: students double-booked in two places at once, assigning beyond a clinical site's capacity, or placing students into clinicals when they haven’t met pre-reqs. Stakeholder: Templates for typical rotations would be helpful. Also, if a conflict occurs, the system could suggest alternative slots or sites with capacity instead of leaving the admin to hunt around. Anything that reduces the back-and-forth.

Stakeholder: It needs to be reliable when our office is open. If it’s down during business hours it causes chaos.
