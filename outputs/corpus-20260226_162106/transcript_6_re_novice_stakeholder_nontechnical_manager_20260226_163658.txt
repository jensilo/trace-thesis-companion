Oh, and we have offices in a few countries, so rollout will be phased over a couple of releases.

Stakeholder: Mostly regular employees who need rooms, executive assistants who schedule for others, and the facilities or site coordinators who manage rooms and equipment. There are also occasional external participants invited to meetings. That’s the mix that will drive adoption.

Stakeholder: Hmm, I think one theme per organization will solve most cases, but some very large customers might want division-level tweaks. We can start simple, though — low lift for them is the priority.

Stakeholder: We measure it by whether people can reserve a room without asking for help. If most people can do a reservation quickly on their own, that’s success. We don’t want long training or lots of support tickets about basic bookings.

Interviewer: When you say "quickly," how quick is too slow? Any specific time targets we should aim for?

Stakeholder: Yeah, hmm, I’d say we should be aiming for, you know, minutes, not tens of minutes. For the simple reserve-a-room flow, I’d expect something like five minutes as a reasonable maximum for most users. If they can’t do it in that window, they’ll get frustrated.

Interviewer: And who counts as "most users"? First-time users or frequent users?

Stakeholder: Good question. I’m mostly worried about first-time or occasional users — people who don’t book rooms every day. Those folks need to be able to figure it out without training.

Stakeholder: They should get immediate, unambiguous feedback. If you book, I want a clear confirmation on-screen and an email or calendar invite so you know it’s real. If there’s a conflict, show why and suggest alternatives. The point is fewer double-bookings and fewer angry emails to facilities.

Stakeholder: We’ll run quick surveys and usability checks. My gut is that if most users say the app confirms their actions accurately, we’re good. I’d like a high majority — maybe around eighty percent positive on that specific question in surveys.

Stakeholder: Customers will expect us to play nicely with their mail. Some will want the app to send through their own servers so invites and notices come from corporate addresses. Interviewer: That makes sense. Do you have particular email systems in mind that we must work with?

Stakeholder: Yes, the usual corporate stuff — Exchange-based environments and cloud mail services, and plain SMTP relays in some places. We should make calendar invites standard so they show up in users’ calendars.

Interviewer: Okay. You mentioned offices in other countries earlier — what do you expect for language options?

Stakeholder: Users should be able to choose their language. For global customers, language switching has to be easy and we must handle locale details like date and time formats so bookings aren’t misinterpreted. We don’t need every language day one, but the app must support selecting a language and we should plan to add the main market languages early.

Stakeholder: Per-user preference is ideal, although allowing org-level defaulting is useful. People travel and some want English even in other regions, so flexibility matters.

Stakeholder: It’s critical. We have public sector prospects and contracts that will require it, and frankly it’s the right thing to do. Stakeholder: I’d target a widely accepted baseline. I’m thinking the usual corporate standard — the middle level of conformance that most customers expect. We should plan automated checks and some manual audits with real users.

Stakeholder: Sure. It just needs to feel immediate — I hate waiting around. For small interactions, like loading the calendar or making a simple reservation, I’d say if things finish in roughly a few seconds people won’t notice. Maybe five seconds for the small stuff feels right, and almost everything should be done within a little longer than that so there aren’t awkward pauses. We want the user’s train of thought to keep going.

Stakeholder: Perceived time is what matters — what the user experiences. Of course the team will want server metrics, but our acceptance will be based on what users feel.

Stakeholder: I’m not being prescriptive about the exact metric there — engineering can define concurrent sessions versus requests — but the point is capacity planning for bursts. The next release is within our first year after launch; we’ll scale as adoption increases.

Interviewer: Ok, noted. On availability — what uptime expectations do we have?

Stakeholder: We want customers to feel they can rely on it. Downtime should be virtually non-existent, aside from genuine infrastructure failures. In business terms, near-continuous availability is the goal; we’ll be held to a tight standard in SLAs.

Stakeholder: Yes, I’d set a very high bar — the kind of number that gives customers confidence. Think in the high four-nines of availability. But we should also be clear about exclusions, like infrastructure outages or scheduled maintenance.

Stakeholder: Only company employees and external collaborators that the company has approved should get access. No anonymous or public accounts. Also, we need full auditability so every login maps to a known identity; that’s non-negotiable from a compliance point of view.

Stakeholder: Yes — one hundred percent of logins should be by either employees or approved external IDs. If anyone else can get in, that’s a serious issue. We’ll work with customers on provisioning, invitations, and federation options so that’s enforced.

Interviewer: Which authentication methods should we support — should we integrate with corporate single sign-on?

Stakeholder: Absolutely — integrate with their identity systems. Some customers will want federation, others will want direct provisioning. We should support the standard enterprise approaches so it plugs into their existing access controls.

Interviewer: Okay. For acceptance and monitoring, how will we prove we meet these goals — like usability, confirmations, performance, uptime and so on?

Stakeholder: We need dashboards and objective measures. Track booking success rates, response times seen by users, email delivery rates, and uptime. Also, run quick post-action surveys to measure perceived confirmation accuracy. For sign-off, we’ll want a mix of synthetic tests and real-user metrics plus some usability test reports.

Stakeholder: Regular weekly dashboards at first, monthly summaries for SLAs, and product/ops will jointly sign off on release acceptance. I’ll work with ops to define the exact evidence we need for each target.
